Domain Cutover Runbook

Moving harvardintech.com to the new site

What it takes to point the real domain at the site we've built, in the order it has to happen, with the exact values for whoever sits in the GoDaddy console — and what we need from you before anyone can start.

DNS discovery 2026-07-02 · steps S1–S9 · decisions D1–D5 · checks V1–V6 · rollback R1–R3
Nothing here has happened yet.

No DNS record has been read or changed, the site is still gated behind its passphrase, and harvardintech.com still shows the Strikingly site exactly as it did yesterday. Every step below is written in the future tense on purpose. This page exists so the move can be reviewed before it is run, rather than reconstructed afterwards.

What this actually is

The site is already built. This is only about pointing the address at it.

harvardintech.com is served today by Strikingly, the no-code tool the current site was built in. The replacement site is finished and running — it's the one you've been reviewing. Nothing about it changes at cutover. What changes is a handful of settings at the domain registrar, so that typing harvardintech.com lands on the new site instead of the old one.

The technical work is genuinely small. The care is all in sequencing: doing the reversible parts first, proving each one before the next, and keeping the old site alive underneath the whole time so there is always a way back.

The one rule

Email must not break.

@harvardintech.com email runs on HostGator, separately from the website, and it is configured through the same registrar screen we'll be editing. The one action that would do real damage is the tempting one: "replace all records." That would take nadia@harvardintech.com and every other mailbox offline, and mail sent during the outage may simply bounce rather than queue.

Only two things change: the apex A records and the www CNAME. The MX record, the mail. host record, and the SPF TXT record are never touched. Step S7 spells out exactly which lines to edit, and V5 makes proving mail still flows a required step before the cutover is called done — not a thing we check afterwards if someone complains.

D3

The board design gallery becomes public. Keep, gate, or remove?

Blocks S6

There are thirteen homepage design mockups (about 20MB) published at an unguessable URL for the board to look at. They're hidden from search engines but have no passphrase — the unguessable address is the access mechanism, and it's a link that's actively been shared. The moment the site becomes public, that folder ships to harvardintech.com along with everything else. Anyone who has the link — or is forwarded it — keeps working access to abandoned design directions on the organisation's real domain.

  • Leave it — it's already effectively public and nobody is going to guess the URL. Costs nothing, changes nothing, and the board's link keeps working.
  • Move it to the review site — it stays reachable for the board behind the same passphrase they already use, and never lands on the public domain. Their current link breaks and has to be re-sent.
  • Remove it — the mockups have served their purpose and the chosen direction is built. Their link breaks permanently.
What we'd suggest

Move it behind the same passphrase as the review site.

Its unguessable URL is currently its only protection, and it is an active board share, so deleting it breaks a live link someone is using. Gating keeps the link working while making it deliberate.

D5

When do we cut over?

Blocks S7

The switch itself is minutes and can be scheduled tightly, but the day matters for a reason that isn't technical: if something is wrong, we want people available to notice and decide, not to find out on Saturday night.

  • A weekday morning — the whole team is around, everyone can check email straight after, and there's a full working day to react. This is what we'd pick.
  • Right before an event or campaign — worst case; traffic peaks exactly while the change is settling.
  • After a specific milestone — e.g. once the board has signed off, or the content list is closed. Tell us the milestone and we'll work back from it.
What we'd suggest

A weekday morning, at least a week after D1 is answered, and not near anything the team is running.

The switch takes minutes but the confidence window is the rest of the day. A morning leaves working hours to notice and reverse. Cutting over before an event means debugging under time pressure.

What this looks like on a calendar

Most of it happens before the day. The switch itself is minutes.
Beforehand 6 steps

Everything reversible. The live site is untouched throughout — at any point here we can simply stop, and nothing has changed for anyone.

  • S1 Re-check what’s there now 15 minutes
  • S2 Prove it on a real subdomain Minutes to set up, then wait for the certificate
  • S3 Work out where old links should land Half a day
  • S4 Tell the host which domain it’s serving Minutes
  • S5 Take the passphrase off Minutes
  • S6 Clear out what shouldn’t be public An hour
On the day 2 steps

The switch and its checks. Best done on a weekday morning, so there are working hours left to notice anything and reverse it.

  • S7 Point the domain at the new site 30 minutes, plus waiting for the certificate
  • S8 Check it properly 30 minutes
Afterwards 1 step

Several days later, not the same afternoon. While Strikingly is still paid for, going back is a two-minute change.

  • S9 Retire Strikingly Minutes, several days later

What it feels like on the day

Minutes of risk, not hours — and a way back the whole time.
  • The switch itself takes a few minutes. A day beforehand we shorten how long the internet is allowed to remember the old address (from hours to five minutes), so when we do flip it, it takes effect almost immediately instead of trickling out over an afternoon.
  • Some visitors see the old site briefly. During those few minutes, whether someone gets the old or new site depends on their internet provider's cache. Both sites work, so nobody sees an error — some people just see yesterday's page for a moment.
  • The certificate takes a little longer. GitHub issues the HTTPS certificate for the domain after the records point at it — typically minutes, occasionally up to an hour. We wait for it before turning on "Enforce HTTPS", so nobody meets a browser warning.
  • We stage on a real subdomain first. Before the apex flips, the new site runs on review.harvardintech.com with a real certificate, so we've seen it work on the real domain (S2) before anything visitors use is touched.
  • Strikingly stays paid for several days after. Rollback is then just putting the old records back (R1–R3), with nothing to re-create or re-configure. We only cancel once the new site has been stable for a few days (S9).

If it goes wrong

Gets weaker with search engines than with people: a static host can’t issue a real redirect, so Google treats it far more weakly than a permanent move. Some ranking on changed paths is lost. Humans following old links land correctly.

Operator detail

Crawl the live Strikingly site and build an old-path → new-path table in a new src/lib/redirects.ts, with a pure lookup function so the map is unit-testable and reviewable as data. scripts/extract.mjs already drives Playwright against https://www.harvardintech.com/ and can be adapted to dump the link graph rather than screenshot sections. Paths that map 1:1 (/, /events, /blog) need no entry.

src/pages/404.astro then looks up location.pathname and redirects on a hit, keeping its existing "go back home" markup for misses.

The accepted loss: GitHub Pages cannot serve a server-side 301, so this is a soft redirect — a 200 page that then moves the browser. Humans following an old link land correctly; search engines treat it far more weakly than a 301, so some ranking on changed paths is lost. The alternative is putting Cloudflare in front purely for redirect rules, which is a permanent moving part for a one-time problem. Worth revisiting only if the crawl turns up genuinely high-traffic paths that changed.

Checks before we call it done

Six things to confirm. V5 is the one that matters most.
  • V1 Both harvardintech.com and www.harvardintech.com serve the new site over HTTPS, with no certificate warning in a fresh browser.
  • V2 robots.txt reads Allow: / and points at a Sitemap: URL on the real domain — not the old placeholder, and not the gated track's Disallow.
  • V3 sitemap-index.xml lists real harvardintech.com URLs, and llms.txt emits the real origin.
  • V4 A sample of old Strikingly URLs from the S3 crawl land somewhere sensible rather than on a bare 404.
  • V5 Send a real email to and from an @harvardintech.com address. Both directions, before declaring the cutover done — not after someone reports it. This is the check that catches the one mistake that actually costs something.
  • V6 review.harvardintech.com still resolves and is still gated. The public launch must not disturb the review track — and it's easy not to notice that it did, because nobody visits it on cutover day. Also confirm the Search Console verification tag survived; it lives in the CMS "Custom <head> HTML" field, not in the code.

If something's wrong

Putting it back is three values, and they're written here on purpose.

Rollback works because Strikingly stays alive until S9 and because the TTLs were lowered in S7 — so reverting propagates in minutes, the same as the switch did. These are the values as of the 2026-07-02 discovery; S1 re-confirms them, and whatever S1 finds is what belongs here.

  • R1 Apex harvardintech.com → A 54.183.102.22 (Strikingly). Remove the four GitHub A records.
  • R2 www → CNAME www.harvardintech.com.s.strikinglydns.com.
  • R3 Leave MX, mail., and TXT alone — as in the cutover, so they are alone in the rollback. If email has broken, these records are where to look, and the fix is restoring exactly what S1 captured.
